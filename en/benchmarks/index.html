Formula & sources

Accuracy = 100 − |estimate − weighed| ÷ weighed × 100. Scores are averaged per item. The 90% shown here is an example of the formula, not the report average.

The reference datasets contain 644 plates and foods. Sample sizes for the reported comparisons are listed with each result.

Nutrition5k © Google Research, CC BY 4.0 (Thames et al., CVPR 2021); USDA FoodData Central; 0xCal’s published benchmark data.

How we test

Three rules, applied the same way to every app on this page, ours included.

  1. Weighed truth

    Every plate's ingredients were weighed by Google Research, and every described food has a USDA reference value. Nothing is scored against an estimate.

  2. One run, no edits

    Each photo goes through the same API the DietFit app uses, each description through DietFit's text analysis. Once, with no retries for a better answer and no manual fixes.

  3. Same formula for everyone

    Accuracy is 100 minus the percentage error, averaged per item. It is the formula 0xCal publishes, applied to every app's answers.

A few things you might wonder

Answers about our accuracy scores and how we test. Still have a question? Get in touch with us.

Is DietFit the most accurate calorie tracker?

On every test we could run head to head, DietFit is at the top or level with it: 82.0% against Cal AI's 80.0% and 0xCal's 79.2% on 0xCal's own photo test, and well ahead of MyFitnessPal and Fitatu on text. We don't claim "most accurate" outright, because no app publishes enough data to test them all on the same plates.

What does the accuracy score mean?

For each plate or food, 100 minus the percentage error: 450 kcal against a true 500 kcal is 10% off, a score of 90. We report the average across items, as 0xCal does.

Can I check these numbers myself?

Yes. Both test sets are public: Nutrition5k from Google Research, with every plate's weighed ingredients, and 0xCal's published benchmark, with every food and every app's answer.
